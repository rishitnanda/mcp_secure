Part A  Should You Write a Research Paper?
The honest answer: Yes — but not right now, and not alone.
Here is the full picture so you can make an informed decision, not a hype-driven one.

Why you SHOULD eventually write one
    • The gap is real and citable. Maloyan & Namiot (Jan 2026) is the first formal MCP security analysis. A paper demonstrating a working open-source implementation of their ATTESTMCP proposals — with live attack success rate measurements — is a genuine contribution, not just a student project.
    • You have the right primary source. You have the actual paper, you understand the threat model, and your suite directly operationalises their proposed mitigations. That is a coherent research narrative.
    • arXiv is accessible to undergraduates. You do not need a supervisor or affiliation to upload to arXiv. A well-written technical report there, even unreviewed, is cited by industry people and shows up in Google Scholar.
    • It compounds. A GitHub repo is a portfolio item. A GitHub repo + arXiv preprint is a research credential. Same work, much larger signal.

Why you should NOT rush it
    • Writing before building is backward. If you start writing now, you will spend three weeks describing a system that doesn't exist yet. Write the code first. The paper writes itself once you have real numbers.
    • A weak paper is worse than no paper. A 4-page preprint with no experimental results, vague claims, and no comparison baseline will be ignored or — worse — noticed for the wrong reasons by a reviewer at a company.
    • You are in your first year. The honest signal here is the code and the GitHub. A paper at this stage is icing, not the cake. Build the cake first.

Decision: Start the paper in Week 3 once you have test results. Publish to arXiv after Week 3 ends, or keep it as a technical report linked from the README. Either is fine. Do not let writing distract from building.

What the paper should look like (when you do write it)

Section	Content
Abstract	2 sentences problem, 1 sentence approach, 2 numbers (baseline ASR vs. your suite's ASR). Write this last.
1  Introduction	MCP adoption stats → why the protocol is a security blind spot → your 3 contributions: (1) open-source implementation of ATTESTMCP, (2) working sandbox, (3) empirical validation against the paper's attack scenarios.
2  Background	MCP architecture in 0.5 pages. The 3 protocol vulnerabilities from the paper (cite Maloyan & Namiot). One paragraph on why existing tools are insufficient.
3  System Design	Shield architecture (engine/transport split, 3-stage policy, ATTESTMCP cert flow). Box architecture (Docker watchdog, cleanup, resource caps). The handoff protocol between them.
4  Implementation	Key decisions you made and why: HTTP/SSE primary transport, auto-deny sampling, per-server config, PSK key management, pre-baked Alpine image. Be concise — this is not a tutorial.
5  Evaluation	This is the core. Run the paper's documented attack types (at least 30 cases). Show baseline MCP ASR (replicate the paper's numbers on your mock servers). Show your suite's ASR. Put this in a table matching Table X of the original paper so comparison is direct.
6  Limitations	Container escape risk (OS namespace vs. hardware VM). PSK vs. ephemeral key exchange. TOFU first-contact gap. Alert fatigue in consent prompts. Being honest here is a sign of maturity, not weakness.
7  Conclusion	Restate contributions, give your headline number, one sentence on future work (Firecracker backend, federated CA).
References	Maloyan & Namiot 2026, AgentDojo, InjecAgent, OWASP LLM Top 10, MCPSecBench, MCPGuard. 10–15 references is fine for an arXiv preprint.

Target length: 6–8 pages in IEEE two-column format. That is the right size for an arXiv preprint that gets read. Under 5 pages looks thin; over 10 pages for a solo first-year project invites skepticism.

Where to submit (in order of effort)
    • arXiv cs.CR — zero barrier, immediate visibility, citable DOI. Do this first.
    • USENIX Security Symposium Student Papers track — competitive but prestigious if you can get a faculty co-author to sign off.
    • IEEE S&P Student Workshop — similar to above, annual deadline in December.
    • ACM CCS Student Research Competition — poster format, good for networking.
    • Do NOT submit to predatory journals (IJCSNS, IJET, etc.). They will accept anything for a fee and actively hurt your credibility.
